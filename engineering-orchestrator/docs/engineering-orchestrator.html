Nova Digitus Engineering Orchestrator — Overview

What it is

Engineering Orchestrator is a design workspace where AI agents do the repetitive parts of electronics design, and proven solvers do the physics. You describe what the board needs to do. Agents draft it, check it, and flag problems. You approve every decision.

The AI decides what to run. The solvers decide what's true. You decide what ships.

The result is less time on drafting and re-checking, and more time on the judgment calls only an engineer can make.

How it works

Every result you see comes from a solver, not from the AI's opinion. Agents handle the coordination, and the numbers come from established simulation tools.

  1. You state the requirement in plain terms, such as voltage, load, temperature limit, or enclosure.
  2. The planning agent breaks it into tasks. Each task appears as a card in your review queue, with its reasoning attached.
  3. The drafting agent proposes a design. That could be a topology, a schematic, or a component choice.
  4. The check agent runs it through a solver. Circuits go through ngspice. Electromagnetic and thermal behavior go through OpenEMS and Elmer.
  5. Problems are flagged with evidence. Each flag carries the simulation trace and two or three options to fix it.
  6. You approve or redirect. Nothing moves forward without your sign-off. Every decision, its reason, and its solver output are logged for design review.

Scenario 1: A new design on a Tuesday morning

Two real problems are caught in ten minutes, before layout starts.

Time What happens What you do
9:05 You type: 5V to 3.3V regulator, 2A load, under 40°C rise in a sealed enclosure. State the requirement
9:06 The planning agent splits it into topology, component sizing, and a thermal check. Three cards appear. Scan the plan
9:09 The drafting agent proposes a buck converter. The check agent runs it in ngspice. Ripple is in spec, but the inductor saturates at 1.8A. Flagged red, trace attached. Approve a larger inductor
9:12 The thermal agent runs the update in Elmer. Temperature rise is 46°C. Second flag, two options: add copper pour, or use a higher-efficiency part. Pick copper pour
9:15 Everything re-simulates and passes. Decisions and solver outputs are logged. Move to layout

The thermal step depends on Layer 2, which is next on the build plan. See What's built today.

Scenario 2: The requirement changes mid-project

This scenario is a real ngspice run. The same buck converter was simulated at 2A and 3A, and the check caught two parts over their ratings in one pass.

Step What happens What you do
1 The customer raises the load from 2A to 3A. You update one line in the requirement. Edit the requirement
2 The planning agent traces what depends on load current: the inductor, the high-side switch, and the output capacitor. Three cards reopen. Review the impact list
3 The check agent re-runs ngspice: a 2 ms transient at 500 kHz. Inductor peak current rises from 2.21A to 3.17A, above its 2.8A saturation rating. Flagged. Choose a higher-rated inductor
4 Same run: switch peak current is 3.17A, above its 3.0A rating. Flagged. Choose a higher-rated switch
5 Output voltage and ripple stay in spec, so the capacitor card closes without action. Nothing
6 The new parts keep 4.7 µH, so the waveform is unchanged. Re-checked against the new ratings (4.5A inductor, 5A switch), both pass. Send the change summary

Measured results

Measure At 2A At 3A Limit Result
Output voltage, average 3.247 V 3.221 V 3.20–3.40 V (3.3 V ±3%) Pass
Output ripple, peak to peak 6.9 mV 6.9 mV Under 33 mV (1%) Pass
Inductor peak current 2.21 A 3.17 A 2.8 A saturation Fails at 3A
Switch peak current 2.21 A 3.17 A 3.0 A rating Fails at 3A

The 3A trace peaks above both rating lines every switching cycle. The 2A trace stays well under them.

Simulation setup: 5 V in, fixed 68% duty (open loop), 30 mΩ switch, diode rectifier, 4.7 µH inductor, 22 µF output capacitor with 10 mΩ ESR. The 2.8A and 3.0A ratings are representative values for the check, not a specific part number. A closed-loop controller would hold the output at 3.30 V; the open-loop droop is shown as simulated.

Without this, a load change means re-checking every part by hand and hoping nothing was missed.

Scenario 3: A compliance check before handoff

The design is checked against your rules before it leaves your desk, so reviewers find fewer surprises.

Time What happens What you do
4:30 You mark the design ready for review. Click "Ready"
4:31 The compliance agent checks it against your team's rule set: component derating, clearance and creepage for the voltage class, approved-parts list, and connector pinout conventions. Wait
4:34 Two flags. A capacitor runs at 85% of its voltage rating against your 80% derating rule. One resistor isn't on the approved-parts list. Each flag shows the rule it broke and a compliant alternative. Accept both swaps
4:36 The check agent re-simulates with the new parts. Everything passes. Confirm
4:37 A review packet is generated: requirement, design decisions, solver results, and the compliance report, in one place. Send it to your reviewer

The rule set is yours. Teams load their own derating limits, parts lists, and standards, and the agent checks against those.

The agents, in plain terms

Each agent has one job, and you can see its work at every step.

Agent Its job What you see on screen
Planning Breaks a requirement into tasks and tracks what depends on what Task cards in your review queue, with reasoning
Drafting Proposes topologies, schematics, and parts A draft design, marked "proposed" until you approve
Check Sends every change to the right solver and reads the results Pass or fail, with the simulation trace attached
Thermal Runs heat and airflow analysis on the current design Temperature results and fix options
Compliance Checks the design against your team's rules and standards Flags naming the rule broken and a compliant alternative

None of the agents sign off on anything. That stays with you.
